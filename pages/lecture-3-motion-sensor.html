Cart on a Ramp: From Graphs to the Equations of Motion

Two classes, one investigation. First a self-guided lab with a motion sensor and a cart on a ramp, generating three graphs. Then a lecture where those same three graphs become the equations of motion, built from data instead of handed down on a formula sheet.

Lecture Experiential Activity
01

Day One: Generating the Graphs

I put students in groups of three or four around a Vernier cart-and-track station, each with a motion sensor on an inclined ramp. Before anyone touches the software, I introduce the sensor itself: it sends out a sonar pulse and reads position from the time it takes the echo to come back, and I show its range and where it stops reading reliably. Then I hand them the Cart on a Ramp activity, the third in Vernier's dynamics series, and let them run it themselves in Graphical Analysis on a LabQuest, with me circulating rather than running the class from the front.

This class is a mess, and that's by design. One sensor loses its lock, another group hasn't zeroed the track, someone's cart is sticking on the rail. I help whoever's stuck while three other problems are happening at three other stations, and groups finish at different times with slightly different runs. What every group leaves with, though, is the same set of three graphs: the cart is given a push up the ramp, decelerates, stops for an instant at the top of its travel, then rolls back down by itself, speeding up the whole way. Position, velocity, and acceleration, all recorded against time, all from the same five seconds of one cart rolling.

02

The Three Graphs

Graphical Analysis lets you fit a curve to any stretch of a graph and read the parameters straight off it. I have students do this to the "rolling on its own" section of each graph, the part after the push and before anyone's hand touches the cart again to catch it. That window is roughly 1.35 to 3.85 seconds. Here's one group's set:

Position — Quadratic Fit y = ax² + bx + c

a = 0.226
b = −1.141
c = 1.826
RMSE = 0.004823

Velocity — Linear Fit y = mx + b

m = 0.4524
b = −1.148
r = 0.9989
RMSE = 0.01567

Acceleration — Statistics, x-range 1.35–3.75 s

Samples: 49
Mean: 0.449 m/s²
Std dev: 0.048
Min: 0.304 m/s² @ 1.350 s · Max: 0.512 m/s² @ 1.550 s

Outside that window, the acceleration trace spikes hard, down to around −2.5 m/s² at the push and down again to around −5 m/s² where a hand catches the cart at the bottom. Those spikes are real, but they're not part of the motion I want modeled. I have students exclude them from the fit on purpose: the push and the catch are someone's hand doing something to the cart, not the cart moving under gravity by itself.

03

Day Two: From Models to Physics

The second class starts with those three fits already on the board, exactly as Graphical Analysis reported them: two curves labeled in plain math, y = ax² + bx + c and y = mx + b, plus the mean acceleration. I tell students directly that this is not IB language yet. It's what I'd write on a board in a college course, real physics, before anyone worries about the exam's notation.

Next to the quadratic, I write the general position equation for constant acceleration: \(x(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 + v_it + x_i\). Next to the line, I write velocity for constant acceleration: \(v(t) = at + v_i\). Matched up term by term against the fits, a in the position equation lines up with \(\tfrac{1}{2}a\), b is the initial velocity \(v_i\), and c is the initial position \(x_i\). In the velocity equation, m is the acceleration directly and b is again \(v_i\).

Dimensional Analysis Both fitted coefficients, the quadratic's a and the line's m, carry the same units once you check them: metres over seconds squared. That alone tells you they're both describing an acceleration before any physics is attached to the letters. Compare their sizes and the quadratic's coefficient is just about half the line's: \(2 \times 0.226 = 0.452\), against a measured slope of 0.4524. That factor of two is where the \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) in \(\tfrac{1}{2}at^2\) comes from, read straight off two independent graphs of the same five seconds.

And there's a third number to check it against: the acceleration graph's own mean, 0.449 m/s², measured directly rather than inferred from a fit. Three separate graphs of the same cart, three separate routes to the same acceleration, agreeing to within a couple percent of each other. That agreement is the actual point of the lesson. Students aren't asked to trust that \(x(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 + v_it + x_i\) is correct because I derived it on the board with calculus. They're asked to notice that three different measurements of the same five seconds of rolling all land on the same number, and that the equation is just a compact way of saying so.

04

A Second Model: Constant Velocity

To get a model students can compare against, I run a second demonstration myself, in front of the class rather than at their stations. The same cart has a spring-loaded plunger built into one end. I compress it against a wall at the end of the track and let it fire, sending the cart off at a roughly constant speed until I catch it by hand at the other end.

Position — Linear Fit, x-range 0.80–1.60 s y = mx + b

m = −0.9039
b = 1.618
r = −0.9997
RMSE = 0.005834

Velocity — Statistics, x-range 0.90–1.55 s

Samples: 14
Mean: −0.892 m/s
Std dev: 0.060
Min: −0.933 m/s @ 1.000 s · Max: −0.700 m/s @ 1.550 s

Here the position graph is a straight line, not a curve, which is the whole demonstration: no curvature means no acceleration. The acceleration trace agrees, sitting near zero for the entire middle stretch, except for two sharp transients bracketing it, a dip to about −4.8 m/s² where the spring fires and a spike to about +4.4 m/s² where my hand stops the cart. Same pattern as Day One: the push and the catch are excluded from the model, because they're not the motion being modeled.

On the board, this becomes a second model, written the same way as the first: y = mx + b in math, translated to \(x(t) = vt + x_i\) in physics, with \(v(t)\) constant and \(a(t) = 0\). I put both models on the board side by side, Model 1 for the ramp and Model 2 for the launched cart, so students see that "constant acceleration" and "constant velocity" are just two versions of the same kind of equation, one with the quadratic term and one without it.

05

Translating into IB Language

Only at the end of the class do I make the switch to the notation the exam uses. \(x(t)\) becomes \(s\), \(v_i\) becomes \(u\), and the equations get renamed SUVAT after their five letters: displacement, initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time.

Model 1, constant acceleration: \(x(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 + v_it + x_i\) becomes \(s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\), and \(v(t) = at + v_i\) becomes \(v = u + at\).
Model 2, constant velocity: \(x(t) = vt + x_i\) becomes \(s = ut\), the same equation with \(a = 0\).

The other two SUVAT equations, \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) and \(s = \tfrac{1}{2}(u+v)t\), follow algebraically from these two, so I mention them but don't build them from data the way I built the first two. By the time these equations show up on a formula sheet later in the course, students have already watched a cart generate every term in them.

06

Field Notes

I like this lesson because it gets the equations of motion into the room without a derivation. I don't stand at the board and integrate acceleration to get velocity, then integrate again to get position. I let the cart do that, and the students' own graphs carry the proof: three separate measurements of the same five seconds of rolling, agreeing with each other to within a couple percent. When \(\tfrac{1}{2}at^2\) shows up later in a problem, it isn't a formula they memorized. It's the shape their own position graph made.

Equipment

  • Vernier cart-and-track kit (built-in spring plunger)
  • Motion sensor
  • Inclined ramp, ruled markings
  • LabQuest Mini or LabQuest 3
  • Laptop/tablet, Graphical Analysis

Physics

Two runs of the same cart, two models. Constant acceleration gives a quadratic position graph and a linear velocity graph, \(x(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 + v_it + x_i\). Constant velocity gives a linear position graph and a flat velocity graph, \(x(t) = vt + x_i\). Translated into IB notation, these are the SUVAT equations \(s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\) and \(s = ut\).
