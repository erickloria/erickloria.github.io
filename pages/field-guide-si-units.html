SI Units, Orders of Magnitude & Significant Figures

Half lecture, half activity — a shared system of measurement, a scale that runs from a proton to the observable universe, and how many digits an instrument actually lets you trust.

Lecture Experiential Activity
01

The Problem With Different Rulers

I open with the seven SI base units and ask the class a simple question: why does a shared system of measurement matter at all? The answer I give them isn't hypothetical. I use a Los Angeles Times article about the Mars Climate Orbiter — a spacecraft lost because English-unit data were mistaken for metric data. A whole mission, gone, over a units mismatch. That's the stakes for the rest of the lesson before a single equation shows up.

02

Placing the Universe on One Scale

From SI units we move into powers of ten and scientific notation. I project an orders-of-magnitude scale running from a proton to the observable universe, and students come up to the board with markers to fill it in wherever they can. Nobody fills in the whole thing alone — that's the point. Watching where the board fills in quickly and where it stalls tells me exactly which parts of that range feel intuitive to this particular class and which don't.

03

How Many Digits Can You Actually Trust?

Then we move to two balances side by side — a digital scale and an old sliding-weight balance — and a handful of objects of different mass. Students read a measurement off each and tell me how many digits are actually significant. I push on the sliding scale specifically: how many digits can you physically read off it before you're guessing? That question is what connects the abstract rule ("count the significant figures") to something you can point to on a real instrument. Most students already know the rule. What they don't always have yet is the instinct that the rule comes from what an instrument can actually resolve, not from a table in a textbook.

What We Found A shared system of units isn't a formality — it's the difference between a spacecraft arriving and a spacecraft being lost. Orders of magnitude and scientific notation let you place wildly different scales, from the subatomic to the observable universe, on one line. And significant figures aren't an arbitrary counting rule: they're a direct readout of how much an instrument can actually tell you.
04

Field Notes

Some students are bored out of their minds during the lecture part because they've seen this material before. Others haven't. The board activity changes the pace, and it's useful to see which parts of the scale they fill in quickly and where they get stuck. The significant-figure discussion does something similar — many students know the rules already, but the instruments connect those rules to digits they can actually read from a physical measurement. It's a packed lesson, but it's the one that gets the course moving.

Physics

SI units: the seven base units and why a shared system matters.

Orders of magnitude: powers of ten across scales from the subatomic to the observable universe.

Scientific notation: writing and comparing values across that range.

Significant figures: the digits that can be read from a physical measuring instrument.
