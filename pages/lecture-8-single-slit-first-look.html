Single-Slit Diffraction: First Look

A student's own hair strand in a green laser beam, and a pattern nobody expects until they draw their prediction first.

Demonstration Lecture
01

Setup

I start cold. I ask a student to give me a strand of hair without explaining exactly what I am going to do with it. I use a green laser because its pattern is easier to see clearly in the room than the red laser's.

02

What Happens

I first establish what students normally expect light to do at an obstacle. I point the laser at something large, often a student standing safely in front of the beam, with it kept below eye level, and the light stops as expected. Before I add the hair, students predict what the same beam will do. If the image is difficult to put into words, I give them paper and ask them to draw it. Then the hair goes into the beam and a visible diffraction pattern appears on the wall. The contrast between the large and small obstacles is the point. After about twenty minutes I move to the board. We draw the pattern they saw and establish the shared vocabulary: central maximum, secondary maxima, dark fringes or minima, aperture width, and the distances and angles used to describe the pattern.

03

Field Notes

The prediction matters. Simply asking what will happen does not always reveal much, but asking students to draw makes the image in their heads visible. The demonstration then gives us something concrete to attach the formal language to. I do not need to make the entire lesson unguided: the exploratory part creates the phenomenon, and the lecture gives everyone the same vocabulary and representation for discussing it. The sequence is deliberate: prediction, observation, then formalisation. This pass stays qualitative; the aperture-width relationship is held for the next lesson.

Equipment

  • A student's hair strand
  • Green laser pointer (example)
  • Large opaque obstacle; screen/wall
  • Paper and pencils, for prediction sketches

Physics

A narrow obstacle produces a diffraction pattern like an aperture of comparable width. The observed pattern has a broad central maximum, weaker secondary maxima, and dark minima. The effect becomes noticeable when the obstacle or aperture is small enough for wavelength to matter.
